The Geometry of Ideation

How sheaf theory turns mathematical discovery into a descent problem — fields as a site, theorems as sections, novelty as a cohomological condition, and the tournament as a gluing computation.

Mathematical ideation — the act of conjecturing genuinely new results by connecting ideas across fields — has historically resisted formalization. It appears to require intuition, serendipity, and deep familiarity with disparate bodies of mathematics. JuGeo's ideation engine replaces this impressionistic account with a precise geometric one: given a catalog of mathematical fields equipped with analogy morphisms between them, new theorems are exactly the sections of a presheaf that live on overlaps but not in the image of any single restriction map. This is a cohomological condition, and the machinery of Grothendieck topologies gives us the tools to compute it systematically.

This page develops the conceptual framework from first principles. We describe the ideation site, define local sections and bridge theorems, characterize novelty as a class in \(H^1\), and show how JuGeo's binary tournament is structurally a descent computation. We close with a fully worked example — Poisson Geometry \(\times\) Infinity Categories — tracing each discovered proposition back to its geometric origin.

1. Mathematical Fields as a Site

JuGeo maintains a catalog of 128 mathematical fields. The naive view treats these as a flat list — a menu from which a user selects two fields to pair. The geometric view treats them as the objects of a category, and that categorical structure is what makes systematic discovery possible.

1.1 The Field Category

Define the field category \(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{math}}\) as follows. Objects are mathematical fields:

\[ \Ob(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{math}}) = \{\text{Type Theory},\; \text{Poisson Geometry},\; \text{Infinity Categories},\; \text{Homotopy Theory},\; \ldots\} \]

Morphisms are analogy arrows — structure-preserving maps that witness a meaningful similarity between fields:

\[ \Mor(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{math}}) = \{\text{analogy arrows between fields}\} \]

An analogy arrow \(f : F_1 \to F_2\) exists when there is a faithful translation of the core concepts of \(F_1\) into the language of \(F_2\). The strength of \(f\) — a number in \([0,1]\) — measures how faithful this translation is: how many of \(F_1\)'s theorems survive transport to \(F_2\) without essential loss. Composition of analogy arrows is transitive analogy: if symplectic geometry analogizes to Poisson geometry, and Poisson geometry analogizes to Lie algebras, then there is a (weaker) composed analogy from symplectic geometry to Lie algebras.

1.2 The Grothendieck Topology

The field category acquires a Grothendieck topology \(J\) by declaring when a collection of fields covers a field. Informally: a field \(F\) is covered by a family \(\{F_i\}_{i \in I}\) when every result in \(F\) can be explained by — or transported from — the combined knowledge of the \(F_i\).

Definition 1.1 — Ideation Site

The ideation site is the pair \((\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{math}},\, J)\) where \(J(F)\) consists of all sieves \(S\) on \(F\) such that for every theorem \(\varphi \in F\), there exists \(f : F_i \to F\) in \(S\) and a theorem \(\psi \in F_i\) with \(f(\psi) = \varphi\).

In other words, a sieve covers \(F\) if and only if every theorem in \(F\) has a preimage in some member of the sieve under the transport map \(f\).

This topology is natural. The Weinstein Splitting Theorem, a result in Poisson Geometry, is covered by its connections to symplectic geometry (local normal form), Lie theory (the symmetry reduction), and foliation theory (the leaf structure). A result in algebraic topology is covered by its connections to homotopy theory, category theory, and differential geometry. The covers are not artificial — they reflect the actual mathematical genealogy of the results.

1.3 Sheaves on the Ideation Site

With the site in hand, we can define sheaves. The key sheaf is the theorem presheaf \(\mathcal{T}\): for each field \(F\), the set \(\mathcal{T}(F)\) consists of all theorems (and conjectures, definitions, and lemmas) that legitimately belong to \(F\). The restriction maps \(\mathcal{T}(F) \to \mathcal{T}(F')\) along an analogy arrow \(f : F' \to F\) are the theorem transport maps: they carry a theorem stated in the language of \(F\) into the language of \(F'\), if such a translation is faithful.

The sheaf condition for \(\mathcal{T}\) on the covering \(\{F_i \to F\}\) is exactly:

\[ \mathcal{T}(F) \;\xrightarrow{\;\sim\;}\; \varprojlim\!\left(\prod_{i} \mathcal{T}(F_i) \;\rightrightarrows\; \prod_{i,j} \mathcal{T}(F_i \times_{\mathcal{F}} F_j)\right) \]

This says that a theorem over \(F\) is completely determined by its restrictions to the covering fields, provided those restrictions are mutually compatible on all pairwise overlaps. The pairwise overlaps \(F_i \times_{\mathcal{F}} F_j\) — the fiber products in the field category — are exactly the bridge regions between fields.

2. Theorems as Sections

2.1 Local Sections

A theorem in a mathematical field is a local section of \(\mathcal{T}\) at that field's coordinate. Consider the following examples:

  • The Weinstein Splitting Theorem (every Poisson manifold is locally a product of a symplectic manifold and a Poisson manifold with zero rank at a point) is a section \(\sigma_{\mathrm{WS}} \in \mathcal{T}(\text{Poisson Geometry})\).
  • The Joyal Model Structure (the category of simplicial sets carries a model structure whose fibrant objects are quasi-categories) is a section \(\sigma_{\mathrm{JMS}} \in \mathcal{T}(\text{Infinity Categories})\).
  • The Homotopy Hypothesis (\(\infty\text{-groupoids} \simeq \text{spaces}\)) is a section in \(\mathcal{T}(\text{Homotopy Theory}) \cap \mathcal{T}(\text{Infinity Categories})\).

Each local section lives at a specific field coordinate. The information in the section includes the statement of the theorem, its proof strategy, its known applications, and the trust level assigned to it by JuGeo's trust algebra (typically VERIFIED for classical results, COPILOT_SUGGESTED for LLM-generated conjectures).

2.2 Bridge Theorems as Sections on Overlaps

A bridge theorem is a genuinely new result connecting two fields. In the sheaf language, it is a section that lives on the overlap — the fiber product — of two field coordinates. It exists precisely because the analogy morphism between the fields creates an overlap region in which both fields' structure is simultaneously present.

Definition 2.1 — Bridge Theorem
\[ \text{Bridge theorem} \;\in\; \mathcal{T}(U_i \times_{\mathcal{F}} U_j) \]

A bridge theorem is a section of the theorem presheaf on the fiber product \(U_i \times_{\mathcal{F}} U_j\) of two field coordinates. Its restriction to \(U_i\) (respectively \(U_j\)) is the "projection" of the bridge theorem onto the language and concepts of field \(i\) (respectively \(j\)).

Not every section on an overlap is a bridge theorem in the interesting sense. If \(\sigma \in \mathcal{T}(U_i \times_{\mathcal{F}} U_j)\) is already in the image of \(\res_{U_i}\) — that is, if it is the restriction of a section that already existed at \(U_i\) — then it is not new: it is just an old theorem from \(F_i\) translated into the language of \(F_j\). The genuinely new bridge theorems are those that are not in the image of any restriction map. This is the cohomological condition developed in §3.

2.3 Restriction Maps and Theorem Transport

The restriction map \(\res^{U_i}_{U_i \times U_j} : \mathcal{T}(U_i) \to \mathcal{T}(U_i \times_{\mathcal{F}} U_j)\) is concretely realized as the theorem transport along the analogy morphism \(f_{ij} : U_i \times_{\mathcal{F}} U_j \to U_i\). Given a theorem \(\varphi\) stated in the language of \(F_i\), the transport \(f_{ij}^*(\varphi)\) is the same theorem restated in the joint language of \(F_i\) and \(F_j\) — with the concepts of \(F_j\) available to clarify, specialize, or generalize the original statement.

Transport is not always possible. If the analogy arrow \(f_{ij}\) is weak (low strength), the translation may not be faithful, and the restriction map is partially defined. When the restriction map is undefined on \(\varphi\), the theorem \(\varphi\) cannot be transported, which means the overlap region is richer than either parent field alone — there are things happening at the overlap that neither field can explain individually. Those things are the bridge theorems.

3. Novelty as a Cohomological Condition

3.1 The Cech Complex of the Ideation Cover

Fix two fields \(F_i\) and \(F_j\) with analogy arrow \(f_{ij}\). The overlap \(U_{ij} = F_i \times_{\mathcal{F}} F_j\) is the bridge region. We have restriction maps

\[ \mathcal{T}(F_i) \;\xrightarrow{\;\res^{i}_{ij}\;}\; \mathcal{T}(U_{ij}) \;\xleftarrow{\;\res^{j}_{ij}\;}\; \mathcal{T}(F_j) \]

The image of \(\res^{i}_{ij}\) consists of all theorems at the overlap that can be "explained" by \(F_i\) — they are just translated parent theorems. Similarly for \(\res^{j}_{ij}\). The cokernel of both maps simultaneously is the space of genuinely novel bridge theorems:

\[ \mathrm{Novel}(U_{ij}) \;=\; \mathcal{T}(U_{ij}) \big/ \left(\im(\res^i_{ij}) + \im(\res^j_{ij})\right) \]

This quotient is what lives in \(H^1\). A section \(\sigma \in \mathcal{T}(U_{ij})\) is novel — in the sense that it represents genuinely new mathematical content — if and only if its image in \(\mathrm{Novel}(U_{ij})\) is nonzero.

Theorem 3.1 — Novelty Criterion

A section \(\sigma \in \mathcal{T}(U_{ij})\) is a novel bridge theorem if and only if

\[ \sigma \;\notin\; \im(\res^i_{ij}) \;\cup\; \im(\res^j_{ij}) \]

That is, \(\sigma\) is not the restriction of any theorem from either parent field. In terms of the \(\check{\mathrm{C}}\)ech complex of the cover \(\{F_i, F_j\} \to F_{\mathrm{bridge}}\), the novelty class of \(\sigma\) is

\[ [\sigma] \;\in\; H^1\!\left(\{F_i, F_j\},\, \mathcal{T}\right) \;\setminus\; \{0\} \]

The \(\check{\mathrm{C}}\)ech 1-cocycle condition collapses here to: on the triple overlap \(U_{ij} \times U_{ij}\) (the overlap of the overlap with itself), the section satisfies the cocycle identity trivially since the overlap is a single object. Nontriviality of \([\sigma]\) is exactly the statement that \(\sigma\) is not a coboundary — not of the form \(\res^i_{ij}(\tau) - \res^j_{ij}(\tau')\) for any \(\tau \in \mathcal{T}(F_i),\, \tau' \in \mathcal{T}(F_j)\).

3.2 Why Cross-Tradition Pairings Are Richer

The novelty of the overlap depends critically on the distance between the two fields in the field category. Fields from the same tradition — say, symplectic geometry and contact geometry, both differential-geometric — have overlaps where the restriction maps are nearly surjective. Almost every section at the overlap is the restriction of something already known in one of the parent fields. The \(H^1\) class is small.

Fields from entirely different traditions — differential geometry and higher category theory, or combinatorics and homological algebra — have overlaps where the restriction maps are far from surjective. The concepts of one field have no natural image in the other, so the overlap contains sections that genuinely cannot be explained by either parent. These are the interesting bridge theorems, and they live in a large \(H^1\). This is why JuGeo preferentially pairs fields from different traditions: it is selecting for large \(H^1\), which is where the mathematical gold is.

💡

The 128-field catalog is not a menu — it is a metric space. The distance between two fields in the category (measured by the minimum number of analogy arrows needed to connect them) is inversely proportional to the triviality of their overlap. The ideation engine uses this metric to bias the tournament bracket toward cross-tradition pairings, maximizing the expected size of the \(H^1\) at each round.

3.3 Higher Cohomology and Multi-Field Bridges

The framework extends naturally to \(H^n\) for \(n \geq 2\). A \(H^2\) class on a triple overlap \(U_{ijk}\) represents a result that requires all three fields simultaneously — it cannot be recovered from any pair. Concretely:

\[ H^2\!\left(\{F_i, F_j, F_k\},\, \mathcal{T}\right) \]

captures theorems that are three-way bridge results. JuGeo's tournament structure is designed to find \(H^1\) classes efficiently (the binary tournament), but the synthesized fields it produces in later rounds become the \(F_i\) for the next round's \(H^1\) computation — effectively computing \(H^2\) and beyond through iterated \(H^1\) computations. This is the mathematical content of the recursive structure of the tournament.

4. The Tournament as a Descent Computation

JuGeo's ideation engine runs a binary tournament over the 128 fields. At each round, fields are paired, the pairing is evaluated by an LLM judge, and the winning pair is merged into a synthesized field that advances to the next round. The tournament terminates when one field remains — the "grand synthesis" of all fields.

This is not a metaphor for a mathematical process. It is a descent computation in the precise sheaf-theoretic sense.

4.1 The Descent Diagram

Recall that sheaf descent says: a section over a covered object \(U\) exists if and only if local sections exist on each \(U_i\) and they agree on all pairwise intersections \(U_i \times_U U_j\). The tournament instantiates this as follows:

ROUND 1 — Local Sections Start with theorems in individual fields
Each of the 128 fields contributes its known theorems as local sections \(\sigma_i \in \mathcal{T}(F_i)\). These are the starting points: well-understood results in their home fields, each with trust level VERIFIED or COPILOT_SUGGESTED depending on whether they are classical or LLM-generated.
ROUND 2 — Overlap Check Find bridge theorems at pairwise intersections
Fields are paired. For each pair \((F_i, F_j)\), the engine computes the fiber product \(U_{ij} = F_i \times_{\mathcal{F}} F_j\) and searches for sections \(\sigma_{ij} \in \mathcal{T}(U_{ij})\) that are not in the image of either restriction map. These are the bridge theorem candidates. The LLM evaluates each candidate on six dimensions, computing a novelty score that estimates the size of the \(H^1\) class.
ROUND 3 — Gluing Merge compatible pairs into synthesized fields
If the overlap is rich enough — the novelty score exceeds the configured threshold — the pair \((F_i, F_j)\) is glued into a synthesized field \(F_{ij}\). The synthesized field inherits all the theorems from both parents plus the new bridge theorems discovered at the overlap. This is the gluing step of sheaf descent: from compatible local sections \((\sigma_i, \sigma_j, \sigma_{ij})\), we construct a global section \(\sigma \in \mathcal{T}(F_{ij})\).
ROUND 4 — Recursion Repeat until one field remains
The synthesized fields \(F_{ij}\) become the new local sections for the next round. The tournament repeats: pairs of synthesized fields are combined, new bridge theorems are found at their overlaps, and the winners are glued. Each round computes \(H^1\) at a higher level of abstraction — effectively computing \(H^2, H^3, \ldots\) of the original field category. The final field is the "global section" of the entire discovery process.

4.2 The Judging Step as Gluing Condition

The LLM judge evaluates each pairing on six dimensions:

Leverage — how much new mathematics does the pairing unlock?
Metaphor Richness — how many analogy arrows connect the two fields?
Transportability — can theorems be faithfully translated between fields?
Proof Density — does the overlap contain enough structure to support proofs?
Novelty — is the bridge content outside the image of both restriction maps?
Geometry Fit — does the pairing fit the judgment-geometric framework?

These six dimensions are not ad hoc. They are precisely the conditions that must hold for the gluing step of descent to succeed:

  • Leverage and Proof Density measure whether the fiber product \(U_{ij}\) has enough sections — whether the presheaf value at the overlap is rich. Without them, the global section produced by gluing is vacuous.
  • Transportability measures how well-defined the restriction maps are. If \(\res^i_{ij}\) is poorly defined, the compatibility condition at the overlap is ambiguous, and the gluing may be inconsistent.
  • Novelty directly measures the \(H^1\) class. A high novelty score means the overlap sections are far from the images of the restriction maps — the synthesis is generating genuinely new mathematics.
  • Metaphor Richness measures the density of analogy arrows, which determines the site topology. More arrows means a richer covering, which means descent is more likely to succeed.
  • Geometry Fit checks global coherence: that the combined structure of \(F_i \cup F_j\) fits within the judgment-geometric framework and can be represented as a consistent site.

A pairing "advances" in the tournament if and only if its combined score crosses a threshold — which is exactly the condition that the local sections on \(F_i\) and \(F_j\) are compatible enough to glue into a coherent global section on \(F_{ij}\). The tournament is descent.

5. Metaphors as Morphisms

The MetaphorFinder is JuGeo's subsystem for discovering analogy arrows between fields. Concretely, it takes two field descriptions and produces a list of structure-preserving translations — each one a morphism in \(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{math}}\).

5.1 The Three Morphism Kinds

Analogy arrows are typed according to how faithful the translation is:

  • ANALOGY — a loose structural similarity. The concepts in \(F_1\) "remind one of" the concepts in \(F_2\), but the translation is not fully rigorous. Strength typically in \([0.3, 0.6]\).
  • EMBEDDING — a faithful translation in one direction. The concepts of \(F_1\) embed into \(F_2\) without loss, but \(F_2\) contains structure that has no counterpart in \(F_1\). Strength typically in \([0.6, 0.85]\).
  • ISOMORPHISM — a fully faithful bilateral translation. Every concept and theorem in \(F_1\) corresponds to one in \(F_2\) and vice versa. This is the categorical equivalence of fields. Strength typically in \([0.85, 1.0]\).

5.2 Metaphor Strength and Site Density

The strength \(s(f)\) of an analogy arrow \(f : F_1 \to F_2\) is not just a quality measure — it determines the covering density in the Grothendieck topology. Specifically, the covering sieves in \(J(F)\) are filtered by the total strength of the morphisms in the sieve:

\[ \text{sieve } S \in J(F) \iff \sum_{f \in S} s(f) \;\geq\; \tau \]

where \(\tau\) is the coverage threshold (default 0.7). A collection of weak analogies can jointly cover a field even if no single analogy is strong enough alone. This is the sheaf-theoretic analog of "the whole is more than the sum of its parts" — five weak analogies collectively provide a richer bridge than one strong embedding.

The Poisson \(\times\) Infinity pairing has four analogy arrows with strengths approximately \([0.72, 0.68, 0.81, 0.65]\), for a total strength of \(2.86\), well above the threshold. The covering is dense, the fiber product is well-defined, and the descent computation can proceed.

6. Propositions as Judgment 8-Tuples

Every proposition discovered by the ideation engine is recorded as a complete judgment in the JuGeo sense. A judgment is an 8-tuple \(J = (c, \varphi, A, E, O, B, T, \Pi)\) whose components carry the full mathematical and epistemic content of the discovered result.

Definition 6.1 — Ideation Judgment
\[ J \;=\; (c,\; \varphi,\; A,\; E,\; O,\; B,\; T,\; \Pi) \]
Component Symbol Description for ideation judgments
Coordinate c The field coordinate where the theorem lives, e.g. poisson_geometry × infinity_categories. For bridge theorems this is always a fiber product coordinate, not a single-field coordinate.
Formula \(\varphi\) The statement of the theorem or conjecture, in natural mathematical language or in JuGeo's formal assertion language when a formalization is available.
Assertion type A One of THEOREM, LEMMA, DEFINITION, CONJECTURE, or BRIDGE_THEOREM. Bridge theorems are the novel sections in \(H^1\); conjectures are unverified candidates.
Evidence E The evidence bundle: LLM-generated proof sketch, novelty score (estimated \(H^1\) class size), plausibility score from fast solver checks, and any analogical precursors that motivated the conjecture.
Open obligations O The proof obligations that remain unmet. For a COPILOT_SUGGESTED theorem, the open obligation is the full formal proof. For a LEAN_VERIFIED theorem, \(O = \emptyset\).
Obstructions B Known counterexamples or contradictions. If the theorem conflicts with a verified result in the ecology, the obstruction is recorded here and the judgment is flagged for review. An empty \(B\) means no known obstructions.
Trust level T One of COPILOT_SUGGESTED (LLM-generated, unverified), SOLVER_CHECKED (fast symbolic check passed), or LEAN_VERIFIED (full Lean 4 proof exists). Trust is monotone-upgradable but never downgraded without explicit obstruction evidence.
Provenance \(\Pi\) Complete audit trail: which tournament round discovered this theorem, which parent fields \(F_i\) and \(F_j\) were paired, which metaphors motivated the pairing, and the full LLM generation trace. Provenance enables reproducibility and supports trust upgrade.

The judgment tuple is not just bookkeeping. It encodes the geometric structure of the discovery:

  • The coordinate \(c\) records where in the field site the section lives.
  • The evidence \(E\) includes the novelty score, which is the numerical estimate of the \(H^1\) class — the "distance" from both parent images.
  • The open obligations \(O\) track the remaining gap between COPILOT_SUGGESTED and LEAN_VERIFIED trust — the proof burden that would elevate the section from a conjecture to a theorem.
  • The provenance \(\Pi\) records the descent path: the sequence of restriction maps and gluing steps that led to this section, enabling verification that the section is genuinely at the claimed coordinate and not a disguised transport from a parent.

8. Why the Geometric Framework Beats Random Combination

Without the geometric framework, cross-field synthesis reduces to what we might call the cocktail party method: pick two fields that sound interesting together, ask an LLM to combine them, and hope something useful comes out. This works occasionally — combinatorics and topology, algebra and geometry, probability and analysis have all produced serendipitous hybrids — but it is unguided. There is no principled way to know in advance which pairings are promising, how to evaluate the results, or when the synthesis is complete.

The geometric framework answers all three questions:

8.1 Which Pairings Are Promising

The Grothendieck topology tells you which pairings have rich overlaps. Given the site \((\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{math}}, J)\) and a pair \((F_i, F_j)\), compute the fiber product \(U_{ij}\) and estimate the dimension of \(\mathcal{T}(U_{ij})\) relative to the images of the restriction maps. Pairs with large \(\dim(\mathcal{T}(U_{ij})) / (\dim(\im \res^i) + \dim(\im \res^j))\) are the promising ones. This ratio is the excess novelty fraction, and it can be estimated cheaply from the analogy arrow graph before running any expensive LLM computation.

Fields from far apart in the field category have high excess novelty fractions. Fields from the same tradition have low ones. The tournament bracket is constructed to maximize the sum of excess novelty fractions across all rounds — this is the combinatorial optimization problem that the bracket-design algorithm solves.

8.2 Where to Look for Novel Theorems

The cohomology tells you where to look. Specifically:

Proposition 8.1 — \(H^1\) Localization

Let \(\{F_i \to F\}\) be a covering family and \(\mathcal{T}\) the theorem presheaf. Novel bridge theorems are supported in the complement of the images of all restriction maps in \(\mathcal{T}(U_{ij})\). A systematic search for novel theorems can therefore be restricted to the orthogonal complement of \(\bigoplus_i \im(\res^{U_i}_{U_{ij}})\) in \(\mathcal{T}(U_{ij})\).

This is immediate from the definition of \(H^1\): the first cohomology group consists of 1-cocycles modulo 1-coboundaries. Coboundaries are exactly the sections in the image of restriction maps. So \(H^1\) sits in the complement of the coboundaries.

Practically: rather than asking an LLM "what theorems connect Poisson geometry and infinity categories?", JuGeo asks "what structures exist at the intersection that cannot be expressed in either field alone?" This is a sharper prompt that produces sharper results.

8.3 How Confident to Be in Each Result

The trust algebra tracks confidence through the judgment tuple's trust component \(T\). This is not just a tag — it is a value in the trust lattice with a precise semantics:

\[ \mathtt{NONE} \;\prec\; \mathtt{COPILOT\_SUGGESTED} \;\prec\; \mathtt{SOLVER\_CHECKED} \;\prec\; \mathtt{LEAN\_VERIFIED} \]

Trust is monotone: it can only increase as more evidence is gathered. The upgrade path from COPILOT_SUGGESTED to LEAN_VERIFIED for a bridge theorem is:

  1. Plausibility check: run fast solver checks on the statement, looking for immediate counterexamples. If no counterexample is found, upgrade to SOLVER_CHECKED (partial).
  2. Proof sketch formalization: have the research copilot expand the proof sketch into a structured outline with explicit lemmas. Each sub-lemma becomes a new judgment with its own trust level.
  3. Lean 4 verification: discharge the open obligations by producing a Lean 4 proof. When all sub-lemmas are verified, the parent bridge theorem upgrades to LEAN_VERIFIED.

8.4 When Synthesis Is Complete

The descent condition tells you when synthesis is complete. The tournament produces a synthesized field \(F_{ij}\) from the pair \((F_i, F_j)\). This synthesis is complete — in the sheaf-theoretic sense — if and only if:

  1. Every local section on \(F_i\) and \(F_j\) has been accounted for (either transported to the synthesis or recorded as an obstruction).
  2. The bridge theorems at the overlap \(U_{ij}\) are compatible with the local sections: their restrictions to each parent field agree with the known theorems in that field.
  3. There are no open obstructions: \(B = \emptyset\) for all bridge theorem judgments in the synthesis.

When these three conditions hold, the local sections on \(F_i\) and \(F_j\) plus the bridge theorems at \(U_{ij}\) form a complete gluing datum, and the descent condition is satisfied. The synthesized field \(F_{ij}\) is a well-defined object in the field category, and its theorem sheaf is the sheafification of the presheaf defined by the gluing data.

⚠

Partial synthesis and \(H^1\) obstructions. When the descent condition fails — when some bridge theorem's restriction to a parent field contradicts a known theorem in that field — the failure is recorded as a nonzero class in \(H^1(\mathcal{U}, \mathcal{T})\). This is not an error in the engine; it is information. The obstruction class tells you precisely which aspect of the two fields is incompatible and must be resolved before a consistent synthesis can exist. Often, resolving the obstruction requires discovering an additional lemma that mediates the conflict — a "bridge to the bridge."

9. Practical Ideation: Domains, Problems, and the Geometry of Usefulness

The preceding sections develop ideation over mathematical fields — pure objects whose sections are theorems and whose value is measured by novelty alone. But most real ideation starts from a different place: you have a practical problem (optimize a supply chain, detect financial fraud, design a curriculum) or you are working within a domain (finance, philosophy, medicine) and want to generate ideas that are not just novel but useful. This section develops the full geometric treatment of that setting: what a "domain" is as a site, what a "problem" is as a stalk condition, and how usefulness and novelty intersect as jointly constraining a search space.

The development proceeds in three phases. First (§9.1–9.2), we establish the categorical infrastructure: domains as sites, sub-domains as objects, methodological translations as morphisms, and the solution presheaf as the analogue of the theorem presheaf. Second (§9.3–9.6), we introduce the problem as a geometric object — a point that determines a stalk — and derive the joint novelty-usefulness criterion that characterizes genuinely valuable ideas. Third (§9.7–9.10), we work through concrete examples, develop the graded theory of usefulness, and show how the problem itself functions as a functor that reshapes the search space.

9.1 What Is a Domain? The Domain Site

A domain like "finance" or "philosophy" is not a single object analogous to a mathematical field. It is itself an entire site — a category equipped with a Grothendieck topology. This is the first and most important conceptual shift when moving from pure mathematical ideation to practical ideation: the unit of analysis is no longer a "field" (a flat object with a theorem set) but a site (a structured category with covering families).

Definition 9.1 — Domain Site

A domain site \((\mathcal{D}, J_{\mathcal{D}})\) is a category whose objects are sub-domains (the constituent problem areas, methodologies, and conceptual clusters within the domain) and whose morphisms are methodological translations — maps that carry a technique or framework from one sub-domain to another while preserving its operative structure.

The Grothendieck topology \(J_{\mathcal{D}}\) declares a collection of sub-domains \(\{D_i\}\) to cover a sub-domain \(D\) when every known solution or practice in \(D\) can be derived from or explained by the combined knowledge of the \(D_i\).

A morphism in the domain site is not merely "these two sub-domains are related." It is a concrete, directional translation: a map \(f : D_\alpha \to D_\beta\) that takes a technique or framework native to \(D_\alpha\) and restates it in the language and constraints of \(D_\beta\), preserving the technique's operative logic. The morphism has a strength \(s(f) \in [0,1]\) measuring how much of the source technique's power survives the translation. This is exactly analogous to the analogy arrows of §1, but now the objects are sub-domains within a single practical domain rather than mathematical fields.

Definition 9.1a — Methodological Translation

A methodological translation \(f : D_\alpha \to D_\beta\) between sub-domains consists of:

(i) A concept map \(f_* : \mathrm{Concepts}(D_\alpha) \to \mathrm{Concepts}(D_\beta)\) sending each core concept of \(D_\alpha\) to a concept in \(D_\beta\) that plays a structurally analogous role.

(ii) A technique transport \(f^* : \mathcal{S}(D_\beta) \to \mathcal{S}(D_\alpha)\) (contravariant) that, given a solution stated in the language of \(D_\beta\), produces the corresponding solution in \(D_\alpha\) by applying the concept map in reverse.

(iii) A strength \(s(f) \in [0,1]\) measuring the fraction of \(D_\alpha\)'s solutions that survive transport to \(D_\beta\) and back without essential loss: \(s(f) = |\{x \in \mathcal{S}(D_\alpha) : f^*(f_*(x)) \cong x\}| / |\mathcal{S}(D_\alpha)|\).

💡

Domains are not fields — they are sites of sites. A mathematical field like "Poisson Geometry" is, in the ideation framework, essentially an object with internal structure captured by its theorem set. A practical domain like "finance" is a whole category of sub-objects with morphisms between them. When we pair two domains for ideation, we are not computing a single fiber product \(U_{ij}\) — we are computing a family of fiber products indexed by all pairs of sub-domains. The useful ideas live in specific fibers of this family, determined by the problem at hand.

9.2 The Hierarchical Structure of Domains

Domains are not flat. They have a hierarchical structure: sub-domains decompose into sub-sub-domains, which decompose further, down to the level of individual techniques and results. This hierarchy is itself categorically structured.

Definition 9.2 — Domain Filtration

A domain site \((\mathcal{D}, J_{\mathcal{D}})\) carries a filtration by granularity:

\[ \mathcal{D}^{(0)} \;\subset\; \mathcal{D}^{(1)} \;\subset\; \mathcal{D}^{(2)} \;\subset\; \cdots \;\subset\; \mathcal{D} \]

where \(\mathcal{D}^{(0)}\) contains only the domain itself (the single top-level object), \(\mathcal{D}^{(1)}\) contains its major sub-domains, \(\mathcal{D}^{(2)}\) adds the sub-sub-domains, and so on. The inclusion functors \(\mathcal{D}^{(k)} \hookrightarrow \mathcal{D}^{(k+1)}\) are fully faithful: all morphisms that exist at level \(k\) persist at level \(k+1\), and new morphisms appear between the finer-grained sub-domains.

For finance, the filtration looks like:

  • Level 0: Finance (single object)
  • Level 1: Derivatives Pricing, Risk Management, Market Microstructure, Behavioral Finance, Portfolio Theory, Financial Regulation, Credit Analysis, Insurance Mathematics
  • Level 2: Within Derivatives Pricing: equity options, fixed-income derivatives, exotic options, volatility modeling, numerical methods (finite differences, Monte Carlo). Within Risk Management: market risk, credit risk, operational risk, liquidity risk, model risk. Within Behavioral Finance: prospect theory, mental accounting, limits to arbitrage, social finance. And so on for each level-1 sub-domain.
  • Level 3: Within equity options: American options, Asian options, barrier options, lookback options. Within market risk: historical simulation, parametric VaR, expected shortfall, stress testing. Each of these has its own techniques, its own known solutions, and its own open problems.

The filtration matters for ideation because the right level of granularity depends on the problem. A problem like "improve corporate risk reporting" lives at level 1 (risk management as a whole). A problem like "reduce Monte Carlo variance in barrier option pricing" lives at level 3 (a specific technique within a specific product class). The stalk of the solution presheaf at these two problems involves very different sub-domain neighborhoods, and the ideation search must be conducted at the appropriate level of the filtration.

The methodological translations between sub-domains also have a filtration-respecting structure. A level-1 morphism from derivatives pricing to risk management (stochastic calculus transport) induces a family of level-2 morphisms: volatility modeling maps to risk factor modeling, numerical methods for pricing map to numerical methods for VaR computation, and so on. The induced morphisms are typically stronger than the parent morphism — finer-grained translations are more faithful because they preserve more specific structure:

\[ s(f^{(k+1)}_{ij}) \;\geq\; s(f^{(k)}_{ij}) \]

This monotonicity of strength under refinement is a general phenomenon: the more specific the translation, the more structure it preserves. A vague analogy between "pricing" and "risk" has moderate strength; a precise correspondence between "volatility surface interpolation" and "risk factor surface calibration" has high strength, because the mathematical machinery is nearly identical.

9.3 The Solution Sheaf and What "Useful" Means Geometrically

On a domain site, the fundamental presheaf is not the theorem presheaf \(\mathcal{T}\) but the solution presheaf \(\mathcal{S}\): for each sub-domain \(D\), the set \(\mathcal{S}(D)\) consists of all known techniques, interventions, frameworks, and actionable insights that belong to \(D\). The restriction maps \(\mathcal{S}(D) \to \mathcal{S}(D')\) carry a solution from one sub-domain to another along a methodological translation — the same technique, restated in the language and constraints of the target sub-domain.

The solution presheaf differs from the theorem presheaf in a critical way: its sections have operational semantics. A theorem is a statement with a truth value; a solution is an intervention with an effect. The effect of a solution \(\sigma \in \mathcal{S}(D)\) is not a logical property but a causal one: applying \(\sigma\) to a problem in \(D\) changes the state of the problem. This causal structure means that the solution presheaf carries additional data beyond the algebraic structure of the theorem presheaf:

Definition 9.3 — Solution Presheaf with Effect

The solution presheaf with effect is the pair \((\mathcal{S}, \varepsilon)\) where \(\mathcal{S}\) is the solution presheaf and \(\varepsilon : \mathcal{S} \times \mathcal{P} \to \mathcal{O}\) is the effect map — a natural transformation that, given a solution \(\sigma \in \mathcal{S}(D)\) and a problem \(p \in \mathcal{P}(D)\), produces an outcome \(\varepsilon(\sigma, p) \in \mathcal{O}(D)\) describing the result of applying \(\sigma\) to \(p\).

The presheaf \(\mathcal{O}\) of outcomes is ordered: outcomes can be better or worse. A solution is effective for a problem if the outcome is strictly better than the status quo: \(\varepsilon(\sigma, p) \succ \varepsilon(\emptyset, p)\).

Now introduce the problem. A well-defined practical problem \(p\) is a point in the domain site — more precisely, it determines a stalk. The stalk of \(\mathcal{S}\) at \(p\) is the space of all solutions that are relevant to \(p\):

Definition 9.4 — Problem Stalk
\[ \mathcal{S}_p \;=\; \varinjlim_{D \ni p} \mathcal{S}(D) \]

The stalk \(\mathcal{S}_p\) is the colimit of the solution presheaf over all sub-domains that contain or bear on the problem \(p\). It collects every solution that could conceivably address \(p\), drawn from all sub-domains in which \(p\) participates.

The colimit here is not a formality — it has concrete content. The stalk at \(p\) includes solutions from every sub-domain that bears on \(p\), but it identifies solutions that become equivalent when restricted to \(p\)'s neighborhood. Two techniques from different sub-domains that reduce to the same intervention at \(p\) are identified in the stalk. This identification is precisely the content of the colimit: it quotients by the equivalence relation "agrees on all sufficiently small neighborhoods of \(p\)." The result is the set of genuinely distinct approaches to \(p\), stripped of sub-domain-specific packaging.

Usefulness is a stalk condition. A section \(\sigma \in \mathcal{S}(D)\) is useful for problem \(p\) if and only if its germ at \(p\) is nonzero: the image of \(\sigma\) in the stalk \(\mathcal{S}_p\) is nontrivial. Concretely, this means that \(\sigma\) — when restricted to the neighborhood of \(p\) in the domain site — actually says something about \(p\). A brilliant technique in derivatives pricing has a nonzero germ at the problem "hedge this portfolio's tail risk" but a zero germ at the problem "design a philosophy curriculum" — it is useful for the former and irrelevant to the latter.

More precisely, a section has a zero germ at \(p\) when, upon restricting it to every sub-domain containing \(p\), the resulting solution becomes trivial — it either does not apply (the technique's preconditions are not met in the problem context) or it reduces to something already known at \(p\) (its image in the colimit is zero because it is identified with the null solution). A nonzero germ means the solution genuinely acts at \(p\): it has operational content that survives localization.

This is the geometric content of "useful": it is not a global property of a section but a local one, evaluated at the specific point determined by the problem. Two sections can be equally novel (both live in \(H^1\)) but differ in usefulness because one has a nonzero germ at \(p\) and the other does not. The distinction between "interesting in the abstract" and "useful in practice" is exactly the distinction between a globally nontrivial section and one that is nontrivial at the specific stalk where you need it.

9.4 The Cech Complex for Cross-Domain Ideation

When two domain sites \(\mathcal{D}_1\) and \(\mathcal{D}_2\) are paired for ideation, the analogue of the Cech complex from §3 governs the computation. But the complex is now richer: instead of a single overlap \(U_{ij}\), we have a family of overlaps — one for each pair of sub-domains \((D_\alpha, D_\beta)\) with \(D_\alpha \in \mathcal{D}_1\) and \(D_\beta \in \mathcal{D}_2\).

Fix a problem \(p \in \mathcal{D}_1\) with sub-domain locus \(\mathrm{Loc}(p) = \{D_\alpha \in \mathcal{D}_1 : p \in D_\alpha\}\). The relevant Cech complex is:

Cross-Domain Cech Complex (Problem-Localized)
\[ \prod_{\substack{D_\alpha \in \mathrm{Loc}(p)}} \mathcal{S}(D_\alpha) \;\times\; \prod_{\substack{D_\beta \in \mathcal{D}_2}} \mathcal{S}(D_\beta) \;\rightrightarrows\; \prod_{\substack{D_\alpha \in \mathrm{Loc}(p) \\ D_\beta \in \mathcal{D}_2}} \mathcal{S}(D_\alpha \times D_\beta) \]

The two arrows are the restriction maps from each parent domain to the overlap. The kernel of their difference is the space of compatible pairs of local sections; the cokernel is the space of novel overlap sections — the \(H^1\) of the complex. By restricting to \(\mathrm{Loc}(p)\), we have already localized the computation to the problem's neighborhood.

The localization to \(\mathrm{Loc}(p)\) is the computational payoff of having a well-defined problem. Without it, the Cech complex runs over all pairs of sub-domains — a potentially enormous computation. With it, we only compute overlaps involving sub-domains that bear on \(p\). If \(p\) lives in 3 sub-domains of \(\mathcal{D}_1\) and \(\mathcal{D}_2\) has 10 sub-domains, the complex has \(3 \times 10 = 30\) overlap terms instead of \(|\mathcal{D}_1| \times |\mathcal{D}_2|\) terms. The problem is a computational lens, focusing the search.

9.5 The Intersection of Novel and Useful

We can now state precisely what it means to find a "useful novel idea" for a given problem. It is the intersection of two geometric conditions:

Theorem 9.1 — Useful Novelty Criterion

Let \(\mathcal{D}_1\) and \(\mathcal{D}_2\) be two domain sites (or a domain site and a mathematical field site) with overlap \(U_{12} = \mathcal{D}_1 \times \mathcal{D}_2\). Let \(p\) be a problem in \(\mathcal{D}_1\). A section \(\sigma \in \mathcal{S}(U_{12})\) is a useful novel idea for \(p\) if and only if:

\[ \underbrace{[\sigma] \neq 0 \;\in\; H^1\!\left(\{D_1, D_2\},\, \mathcal{S}\right)}_{\text{novelty: not in image of either restriction}} \quad\text{and}\quad \underbrace{\sigma_p \neq 0 \;\in\; \mathcal{S}_p}_{\text{usefulness: nonzero germ at } p} \]

The novelty condition is exactly as in §3 — the section is not obtainable by transporting a known solution from either parent domain alone. The usefulness condition adds that the section must survive localization to the problem point. The conjunction of these two conditions carves out a subspace of \(\mathcal{S}(U_{12})\) that is generically smaller than either condition alone — useful novelty is rarer than novelty or usefulness individually, which accords with the empirical observation that most novel ideas are impractical and most practical ideas are unoriginal.

The two conditions interact in a geometrically precise way. Consider the space \(\mathcal{S}(U_{12})\) of all sections on the overlap. It decomposes into four regions:

Useful (\(\sigma_p \neq 0\)) Not useful (\(\sigma_p = 0\))
Novel (\([\sigma] \neq 0\)) Useful novel ideas. The target. Sections in \(H^1 \cap \{\sigma_p \neq 0\}\). These are the ideas that are both genuinely new (not transportable from either parent) and bear concretely on the problem at hand. Clever but irrelevant. Sections in \(H^1\) that have zero germ at \(p\). These are intellectually interesting cross-domain connections that happen not to address the problem. They may be valuable for other problems, but not this one.
Not novel (\([\sigma] = 0\)) Useful but unoriginal. Sections in \(\im(\res^1) \cup \im(\res^2)\) with nonzero germ at \(p\). These are known techniques from one parent domain, transported to the overlap. They address the problem but add nothing new — they are "conventional wisdom" restated in cross-domain language. Irrelevant and unoriginal. The intersection of both failure modes. Most of the overlap consists of such sections — routine knowledge that neither bears on the specific problem nor contains genuinely new insight.

The target — the upper-left cell — is generically the smallest of the four regions. This is a geometric necessity: the novelty condition removes the images of both restriction maps (which are typically large subspaces), and the usefulness condition further restricts to the fiber over \(p\) (which is typically a small subspace of what remains). The intersection is generically transverse: the novelty condition and the usefulness condition cut independently across \(\mathcal{S}(U_{12})\), so the dimension of the useful-novel subspace is roughly

\[ \dim\!\left(H^1 \cap \{\sigma_p \neq 0\}\right) \;\approx\; \dim(H^1) \cdot \frac{\dim(\mathcal{S}_p)}{\dim(\mathcal{S}(U_{12}))} \]

When the two conditions are not transverse — when the novel sections tend to cluster at or away from the problem point — the geometry tells you something important. If novel sections avoid the problem point (sub-transverse intersection), the problem is one that the cross-domain pairing cannot help with: the interesting connections between the domains happen to be irrelevant to this specific problem. You should try a different partner domain. If novel sections concentrate at the problem point (super-transverse intersection), the problem is a natural locus for cross-domain innovation: the domains' overlap is richest precisely where the problem lives. This is the ideal case, and it occurs when the problem itself embodies the structural tension between the two domains.

This is the central geometric insight for practical ideation: a well-defined problem does not make ideation harder — it makes it more tractable. Without the problem, you must search all of \(H^1(U_{12}, \mathcal{S})\) for novel sections and then evaluate each for relevance post hoc. With the problem, you search only in the fiber of \(H^1\) over the stalk at \(p\) — a dramatically smaller space. The problem acts as a localization that collapses the search space from the full cohomology group to a single stalk's worth of it.

9.6 Cross-Domain Ideation in Practice

The practical procedure for finding useful ideas given a problem \(p\) in domain \(\mathcal{D}_1\) is:

  1. Identify the problem's sub-domain locus. Determine which sub-domains of \(\mathcal{D}_1\) contain \(p\) — which sub-domains have nonzero relevance to the problem. This determines the stalk neighborhoods over which you will compute, and the level of the domain filtration (§9.2) at which to conduct the search. A level-1 problem ("improve risk management") requires level-1 sub-domain loci; a level-3 problem ("reduce variance in barrier option Monte Carlo") requires level-3 loci. Mismatching the level produces either too-vague ideas (problem is specific, search is coarse) or too-narrow ideas (problem is broad, search is fine-grained).

    The locus computation is itself a geometric operation: it is the support of the problem's image in the demand sheaf \(\mathcal{P}\). The support \(\mathrm{Supp}(p) = \{D \in \mathcal{D}_1 : p|_D \neq 0\}\) is the set of all sub-domains where the problem has nonzero projection. For example, the problem "reduce recidivism in criminal sentencing" has support in {sentencing theory, rehabilitative policy, criminology, sociology of punishment} but not in {intellectual property, maritime law, contract law}. The support is typically a connected sub-category of \(\mathcal{D}_1\) — the sub-domains bearing on the problem are methodologically linked to each other.

  2. Select a partner domain \(\mathcal{D}_2\) with high excess novelty. The same metric as §8.1 applies: compute the fiber product of the sub-domain neighborhoods of \(p\) with the sub-domains of \(\mathcal{D}_2\), and estimate the excess novelty fraction. Partner domains whose methodological translations are structurally rich but linguistically distant from \(\mathcal{D}_1\) will produce the largest \(H^1\).

    Concretely, the excess novelty fraction for the pairing \((\mathrm{Loc}(p), \mathcal{D}_2)\) is:

    \[ \mathrm{ENF}(p, \mathcal{D}_2) \;=\; \frac{\dim \mathcal{S}(U_{12}) \;-\; \dim(\im \res^1 + \im \res^2)} {\dim \mathcal{S}(U_{12})} \]

    An ENF close to 1 means almost all the overlap content is novel — the two domains have a rich intersection but almost no shared knowledge. An ENF close to 0 means the overlap is dominated by transported parent knowledge — the domains are too similar for productive ideation. Empirically, the sweet spot is \(\mathrm{ENF} \in [0.4, 0.8]\): enough structure for the fiber product to be well-defined, but enough distance for the \(H^1\) to be large. Pairing criminal justice with behavioral economics yields \(\mathrm{ENF} \approx 0.3\) (the fields already talk to each other); pairing it with ecology yields \(\mathrm{ENF} \approx 0.7\); pairing it with abstract algebra yields \(\mathrm{ENF} \approx 0.95\) (too distant — the fiber product is nearly empty because the morphisms are too weak).

  3. Search \(H^1\) in the fiber over \(p\). On the overlap \(U_{12}\), look for sections that (a) are not in the image of either restriction map and (b) have nonzero germ at \(p\). This is the constrained search: the LLM is prompted not with "what connects domain 1 and domain 2?" but with "what structures at the intersection of domain 1 and domain 2 bear on problem \(p\) and cannot be expressed in either domain alone?"

    The prompt structure encodes the geometric constraints directly:

    • The novelty constraint is encoded by the instruction "cannot be expressed in either domain alone" — this is the \(H^1\) condition, asking for sections outside both restriction-map images.
    • The usefulness constraint is encoded by the instruction "bear on problem \(p\)" — this is the stalk condition, asking for nonzero germ at \(p\).
    • The overlap structure is encoded by providing the specific analogy morphisms between sub-domains of \(\mathrm{Loc}(p)\) and sub-domains of \(\mathcal{D}_2\) — this gives the LLM the concrete concept maps along which to search for novel sections.
  4. Evaluate via the judgment tuple. Each candidate is recorded as a judgment \(J = (c, \varphi, A, E, O, B, T, \Pi)\) exactly as in §6, but now the evidence bundle \(E\) includes both a novelty score (estimating \(|[\sigma]|\) in \(H^1\)) and a relevance score (estimating \(|\sigma_p|\) in the stalk). The product of these two scores is the useful novelty score — the single number that ranks ideas by their joint satisfaction of both conditions.

    The useful novelty score has a precise geometric interpretation:

    \[ \mathrm{UNS}(\sigma, p) \;=\; \underbrace{\mathrm{nov}(\sigma)}_{\text{novelty}} \;\cdot\; \underbrace{\mathrm{rel}(\sigma, p)}_{\text{relevance}} \;=\; \|[\sigma]\|_{H^1} \;\cdot\; \|\sigma_p\|_{\mathcal{S}_p} \]

    The multiplicative structure is not arbitrary: a section with high novelty and zero relevance scores 0 (clever but useless); a section with zero novelty and high relevance scores 0 (useful but known); only sections with both properties score positively. The product is the natural norm on the useful-novel subspace.

9.7 What Makes a "Good" Domain Pairing: The Morphism Structure

Not all cross-domain pairings are equally productive. The geometry tells you why. The fiber product \(U_{12} = \mathcal{D}_1 \times \mathcal{D}_2\) is rich — meaning it has many sections, meaning there are many potential ideas at the intersection — when the analogy morphisms between sub-domains are structurally faithful but semantically surprising. This is a precise condition:

Proposition 9.2 — Productive Pairing Criterion

A cross-domain pairing \((\mathcal{D}_1, \mathcal{D}_2)\) is productive for problem \(p\) when the analogy morphisms between the sub-domains of \(\mathcal{D}_1\) containing \(p\) and the sub-domains of \(\mathcal{D}_2\) satisfy:

\[ \underbrace{s(f_{ij}) \geq \tau_{\min}}_{\text{structural faithfulness}} \quad\text{and}\quad \underbrace{d_{\mathcal{F}}(D_i, D_j) \geq \delta_{\min}}_{\text{semantic distance}} \]

where \(s(f_{ij})\) is the morphism strength and \(d_{\mathcal{F}}(D_i, D_j)\) is the shortest-path distance in the global domain category. High strength ensures the translation is well-defined (the restriction maps exist and are faithful); high distance ensures the translation is surprising (the sections at the overlap are far from both restriction-map images).

The condition can be visualized in the strength-distance plane. Every potential cross-domain morphism \(f : D_\alpha \to D_\beta\) is a point \((s(f), d(D_\alpha, D_\beta))\) in \([0,1] \times \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\). The productive region is the upper-right quadrant: \(s \geq \tau_{\min}\) and \(d \geq \delta_{\min}\). Morphisms in the lower-right quadrant (high distance, low strength) are aspirational analogies — the domains are far apart but the translation is too weak to support a well-defined fiber product. Morphisms in the upper-left quadrant (high strength, low distance) are routine translations — the domains are close enough that the overlap is mostly transported parent knowledge. Only the upper-right quadrant produces both a well-defined overlap and a large \(H^1\).

This criterion explains a well-known empirical pattern: the most productive cross-domain ideas come from fields that are structurally similar but culturally unrelated. Ecology and economics share deep structural parallels (population dynamics ↔ market dynamics, carrying capacity ↔ market saturation, predator-prey ↔ competition) but developed independently and use different languages. The analogy arrows are strong (\(s \geq 0.7\)) but the cultural distance is large (\(d \geq 4\) in the global domain graph). This is the sweet spot: the fiber product is well-defined (strong morphisms) and its \(H^1\) is large (high distance).

Contrast this with pairing two sub-fields of the same domain — say, derivatives pricing and risk management within finance. The morphisms are strong (\(s \approx 0.9\)) but the distance is small (\(d = 1\)): they are already deeply intertwined. The restriction maps are nearly surjective, the \(H^1\) is small, and the "novel" ideas at the overlap are mostly rephrasing of known techniques. Productive ideation requires escaping the local neighborhood in the domain graph.

9.8 The Demand Sheaf: Encoding What Problems Need

To make the stalk condition fully rigorous, we introduce a second sheaf on the domain site: the demand sheaf \(\mathcal{P}\). For each sub-domain \(D\), the set \(\mathcal{P}(D)\) consists of the open problems in \(D\) — the unsolved questions, unmet needs, and known gaps in current practice. A well-defined practical problem \(p\) is a global section of \(\mathcal{P}\) (it lives in a specific sub-domain but may have projections to several others).

The demand sheaf has its own restriction maps: if \(f : D_\alpha \to D_\beta\) is a methodological translation, then \(\res^{\mathcal{P}}_f : \mathcal{P}(D_\beta) \to \mathcal{P}(D_\alpha)\) transports an open problem from \(D_\beta\) to \(D_\alpha\). The transported problem is the "projection" of the original problem into the language of \(D_\alpha\). A financial regulation problem like "ensure systemic stability" restricts along the morphism to credit analysis as "ensure adequate recovery rates," and restricts along the morphism to behavioral finance as "counteract herding behavior in crisis." Each restriction captures a facet of the original problem, but no single restriction captures the whole — this is why the problem requires the full covering family, not just one sub-domain.

Usefulness is then the pairing of the solution sheaf with the demand sheaf:

Definition 9.5 — Usefulness Pairing
\[ \langle \sigma, p \rangle \;:\; \mathcal{S}(U) \times \mathcal{P}(U) \;\to\; [0, 1] \]

A section \(\sigma \in \mathcal{S}(U)\) is useful for \(p \in \mathcal{P}(U)\) when \(\langle \sigma, p \rangle > 0\): the solution addresses the problem. The value \(\langle \sigma, p \rangle \in [0,1]\) measures the degree of usefulness — how much of the problem's demand is met by the solution. This pairing is local — it is defined on each open set of the domain site — and the sheaf condition ensures that if a solution is locally useful on every sub-domain containing \(p\), it is globally useful for \(p\).

The pairing \(\langle \sigma, p \rangle\) is a geometric version of "does this idea actually help?" It factors through the stalk: a section is useful for \(p\) if and only if its germ at \(p\) pairs nontrivially with the germ of \(p\) in the demand sheaf. The full useful novelty condition from Theorem 9.1 is then: \(\sigma\) lives in \(H^1\) (novelty) and pairs nontrivially with \(p\) (usefulness). This is a joint constraint on a single search space, not two independent filters applied sequentially.

The pairing has a compatibility property with restriction maps that is essential for coherence: if \(\sigma\) is useful for \(p\) on \(U\), and we restrict both \(\sigma\) and \(p\) to a sub-domain \(D \subset U\), the restricted solution should still be useful for the restricted problem:

\[ \langle \res_D(\sigma),\, \res_D(p) \rangle \;\geq\; \langle \sigma, p \rangle \]

Restriction can only increase the pairing, because restricting to a smaller neighborhood focuses both the solution and the problem onto their shared region of relevance, discarding irrelevant components of both. This monotonicity under restriction is the sheaf-theoretic expression of a commonsense principle: a good idea looks even better when you focus on exactly the aspect of the problem it addresses.

9.10 Graded Usefulness and the Relevance Filtration

The binary version of usefulness — germ is zero or nonzero — is a first approximation. In practice, usefulness admits degrees: some ideas are more useful than others for the same problem. The geometry accounts for this through a filtration on the stalk.

Definition 9.6 — Relevance Filtration

The stalk \(\mathcal{S}_p\) carries a relevance filtration:

\[ 0 \;=\; F^0 \mathcal{S}_p \;\subset\; F^1 \mathcal{S}_p \;\subset\; F^2 \mathcal{S}_p \;\subset\; \cdots \;\subset\; \mathcal{S}_p \]

where \(F^k \mathcal{S}_p\) consists of all germs whose relevance to \(p\) is at level \(k\) or below:

\(F^1\): tangentially relevant — the solution addresses a problem in the same sub-domain as \(p\) but not \(p\) itself.

\(F^2\): partially relevant — the solution addresses one component or aspect of \(p\) but not the full problem.

\(F^3\): directly relevant — the solution addresses \(p\) as stated, providing a concrete intervention or technique.

\(F^4\): transformatively relevant — the solution not only addresses \(p\) but reframes it in a way that dissolves the original tension, revealing that the problem was a special case of a deeper structure.

The filtration level determines the quality of the idea, not just its existence. A level-1 germ ("this ecology concept is vaguely related to criminal justice") is tangential; a level-4 germ ("the recidivism-proportionality tradeoff is a special case of the conservation-harvest Pareto frontier, and the entire efficient-frontier machinery transfers") is transformative. The useful novelty score from §9.6 should be weighted by filtration level:

\[ \mathrm{UNS}_{\mathrm{graded}}(\sigma, p) \;=\; \mathrm{nov}(\sigma) \;\cdot\; k(\sigma, p) \;\cdot\; \mathrm{rel}(\sigma, p) \]

where \(k(\sigma, p) \in \{1, 2, 3, 4\}\) is the filtration level of \(\sigma\)'s germ at \(p\). The multiplicative factor amplifies transformatively relevant ideas (level 4) relative to tangentially relevant ones (level 1), even if both have the same raw relevance score. In the worked example above, the Recidivism-Proportionality Pareto Frontier proposition is at filtration level 4 (it reframes the problem as a Pareto optimization), while the Mark-Recapture Equivalence is at level 2 (it addresses the data component of the problem but not the policy design).

9.11 The Problem Functor: How Problems Reshape the Search Space

The deepest insight of the geometric framework is that the problem is not a passive filter applied after ideation — it is an active functor that reshapes the entire search space before the search begins.

Theorem 9.3 — Problem Functor

Let \(\mathcal{P}\) be the demand sheaf on the domain site \(\mathcal{D}_1\). Each problem \(p \in \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{D}_1)\) determines a functor

\[ \Phi_p \;:\; \mathbf{DomSites} \;\to\; \mathbf{SearchSpaces} \]

that sends each candidate partner domain \(\mathcal{D}_2\) to the localized search space \(\Phi_p(\mathcal{D}_2) = H^1(\mathrm{Loc}(p) \times \mathcal{D}_2,\, \mathcal{S}) \cap \{\sigma_p \neq 0\}\). The functor is contravariant in \(p\) (broadening the problem enlarges the search space) and covariant in \(\mathcal{D}_2\) (enriching the partner domain enriches the search).

The variance properties of the problem functor have practical consequences. Contravariance in \(p\) means that making the problem more specific shrinks the search space: a precise problem like "reduce recidivism among 18–25-year-olds released from medium-security facilities in urban areas" has a much smaller search space than the generic "reduce recidivism." This is a feature, not a bug: the smaller search space concentrates on ideas that are relevant to the precise problem, discarding the vast majority of cross-domain connections that, while intellectually interesting, do not bear on this specific population and context.

Covariance in \(\mathcal{D}_2\) means that enriching the partner domain — adding more sub-domains, refining the filtration level, discovering additional analogy morphisms — enlarges the search space. A crude model of ecology with three sub-domains produces a small \(H^1\); a refined model with twelve sub-domains at level 2 of the filtration produces a much larger \(H^1\), because the finer-grained overlaps contain more sections that are invisible at the coarse level.

The interplay of these two variances determines the optimal operating point for practical ideation: be as specific as possible about the problem and as rich as possible in the partner domain. A specific problem with a rich partner domain produces a search space that is small (computationally tractable) but dense (every element is both novel and relevant). This is the geometric explanation for why the best practical ideas emerge from deep expertise in one domain (you understand the problem at high specificity) combined with broad literacy in another (you have access to many sub-domains and morphisms).

📋

The problem functor unifies "problem-driven" and "domain-driven" ideation. If you start with a well-defined problem \(p\), the functor \(\Phi_p\) tells you which partner domains to explore and where in each partner's site to search. If you start with two domains \(\mathcal{D}_1, \mathcal{D}_2\) and want to know what problems they can jointly address, you invert the functor: compute \(H^1\) for the full (non-localized) overlap, then ask which problems \(p\) have nonzero pairing with the resulting sections. The two modes — "I have a problem, find me an idea" and "I have two domains, find me a problem they jointly solve" — are adjoint operations on the same geometric data.

⚠

Novelty without usefulness is cleverness; usefulness without novelty is incrementalism. The full force of the geometric framework is that it searches for sections satisfying both conditions simultaneously, in a single constrained search over \(H^1 \cap \{\sigma_p \neq 0\}\). This is why the framework produces ideas that are neither "interesting but impractical" nor "practical but obvious" — it is optimizing over the intersection of two sheaf-theoretic conditions, not the union.

10. Composition of Domains and the Geometry of Idea Scale

The preceding sections develop ideation over pairs of domains. But the most powerful ideas often draw on three, four, or more domains simultaneously — and the most substantial ideas (those that justify a large-scale software system rather than a small tweak) have a geometric signature that distinguishes them from minor variations. This section develops the full theory of n-fold domain composition and the geometric invariant that measures idea scale.

10.1 The \(n\)-fold Fiber Product: Composing Arbitrarily Many Domains

There is nothing special about two. The fiber product of two domain sites \(\mathcal{D}_1 \times \mathcal{D}_2\) generalizes immediately to the \(n\)-fold fiber product:

Definition 10.1 — \(n\)-fold Domain Composition

Given domain sites \(\mathcal{D}_1, \ldots, \mathcal{D}_n\), their composition is the \(n\)-fold fiber product

\[ \mathcal{D}_1 \times \mathcal{D}_2 \times \cdots \times \mathcal{D}_n \;=\; \varprojlim_{\substack{i < j}} \left(\mathcal{D}_i \times \mathcal{D}_j\right) \]

An object of this product is a tuple \((D_{\alpha_1}, D_{\alpha_2}, \ldots, D_{\alpha_n})\) of sub-domains, one from each domain site, together with compatible methodological translations between every pair. "Compatible" means the translations compose consistently: if \(f_{12}\) translates \(\mathcal{D}_1 \to \mathcal{D}_2\) and \(f_{23}\) translates \(\mathcal{D}_2 \to \mathcal{D}_3\), then \(f_{13}\) (the direct translation \(\mathcal{D}_1 \to \mathcal{D}_3\)) must agree with the composition \(f_{23} \circ f_{12}\) — this is the cocycle condition on the methodological translations.

The cocycle condition is essential. Without it, the composition is incoherent: you could translate a concept from domain 1 to domain 3 via domain 2 and get a different answer than translating directly. The cocycle condition ensures that the n-fold composition defines a single, well-defined overlap region in which all \(n\) domains' structures are simultaneously present and mutually consistent.

The solution presheaf on the \(n\)-fold product is:

\[ \mathcal{S}\!\left(\mathcal{D}_1 \times \cdots \times \mathcal{D}_n\right) \;=\; \left\{\sigma \;\Big|\; \res^i(\sigma) \in \mathcal{S}(\mathcal{D}_i) \;\text{for all } i,\;\; \res^{ij}(\sigma) \text{ compatible on all pairwise overlaps} \right\} \]

A section of this presheaf is a solution that simultaneously draws on all \(n\) domains and is internally consistent across every pair. This is dramatically more constrained than a pairwise bridge: every additional domain adds \(\binom{n}{2}\) new compatibility conditions, which is why genuine \(n\)-fold compositions are rare and powerful.

10.2 Higher Cohomology: Ideas That Require \(n\) Domains Simultaneously

The cohomological framework extends naturally to multi-domain compositions. The key:

Theorem 10.1 — Higher Cohomology and \(n\)-Domain Ideas

A section \(\sigma\) in the \(n\)-fold overlap is a genuinely \(n\)-domain idea — one that cannot be reduced to any \((n-1)\)-domain combination — if and only if its class in \(H^{n-1}\) is nonzero:

\[ [\sigma] \;\neq\; 0 \;\in\; H^{n-1}\!\left(\{\mathcal{D}_1, \ldots, \mathcal{D}_n\},\; \mathcal{S}\right) \]

A nonzero \(H^1\) class requires 2 domains. A nonzero \(H^2\) class requires 3. In general, a nonzero \(H^{n-1}\) class is an idea that irreducibly requires all \(n\) domains — no subset suffices.

The \(\check{\mathrm{C}}\)ech complex of the cover \(\{\mathcal{D}_1, \ldots, \mathcal{D}_n\}\) has \(\binom{n}{k}\) terms at degree \(k\). The differential maps sections on \(k\)-fold overlaps to \((k+1)\)-fold overlaps. A section in \(H^{n-1}\) is a cocycle at degree \(n-1\) that is not a coboundary — it lives on the full \(n\)-fold overlap but cannot be decomposed as a combination of sections from any proper sub-cover. This is the irreducibility condition.

In practice, most useful ideas are \(H^1\) (two-domain bridges). Some are \(H^2\) (three-domain bridges — these are the ideas that require, say, finance AND optimal transport AND control theory simultaneously, where no pair of these suffices). Genuine \(H^3\) and higher classes are extremely rare and typically indicate foundational breakthroughs.

The tournament structure from §4 already computes higher cohomology through iterated \(H^1\): the synthesized field from round 1 (a two-domain bridge) becomes an object for round 2's \(H^1\) computation, effectively computing \(H^2\) of the original cover. But the \(n\)-fold fiber product makes this explicit: rather than iterating pairwise, we can compose \(n\) domains at once and search the full \(H^{n-1}\) directly.

10.3 Idea Scale as a Geometric Invariant

Not all ideas are equal in scale. A small idea (a single optimization trick, a parameter tweak) can be stated in a paragraph and implemented in a hundred lines. A large idea (a new computational framework, a new way of structuring an entire domain) requires thousands of lines and dozens of interacting components. The question is: can we distinguish these geometrically?

Yes. The scale of an idea is measured by the dimension of its covering family — the number of independent, non-trivial local sections required to cover the idea's support region. This is a topological invariant of the idea, not a stylistic choice.

Definition 10.2 — Idea Scale (Covering Dimension)

Let \(\sigma \in H^{n-1}(\{\mathcal{D}_1, \ldots, \mathcal{D}_n\}, \mathcal{S})\) be a bridge idea. The covering dimension of \(\sigma\) is the minimum cardinality of a covering family \(\{U_\alpha\}_{\alpha \in A}\) of the support of \(\sigma\) in the overlap region:

\[ \mathrm{covdim}(\sigma) \;=\; \min\!\left\{|A| \;\Big|\; \{U_\alpha\}_{\alpha \in A} \text{ covers } \mathrm{Supp}(\sigma),\;\; \sigma|_{U_\alpha} \neq 0 \;\forall \alpha \right\} \]

Each element of the covering family corresponds to an independent component of the idea — a module, a subsystem, a distinct algorithmic contribution — that carries nontrivial content and cannot be merged with the others without loss.

The covering dimension directly predicts the scale of the implementation:

Covering Dimension Idea Scale Implementation Scale Example
1–2 Small tweak ~100–500 lines A new loss function, a parameter choice
3–5 Focused tool ~1,000–5,000 lines A specialized optimizer, a new metric
6–12 Substantial system ~5,000–20,000 lines A new computational framework with pipeline
13+ Platform 20,000+ lines A new paradigm with ecosystem

The key insight: a large covering dimension is not a choice — it is a consequence of the idea's geometric structure. An idea with covering dimension 8 genuinely needs 8 independent components because its support region in the overlap cannot be covered by fewer. Each component addresses a distinct region of the overlap where the source and target domains interact in a unique way. Merging two components into one would either lose information (fail to cover a region) or introduce redundancy (cover a region twice).

Proposition 10.2 — Scale–LOC Correspondence

Let \(\sigma\) be a bridge idea with covering dimension \(\mathrm{covdim}(\sigma) = k\). Then a faithful implementation of \(\sigma\) — one where every covering element maps to a module that fully realizes its local section — requires at least \(\Omega(k \cdot L_{\min})\) lines of code, where \(L_{\min}\) is the minimum lines needed to implement a single nontrivial local section (empirically, \(L_{\min} \approx 500\)–\(1500\) for production Python).

Conversely, if an implementation has \(N\) lines but the underlying idea has \(\mathrm{covdim}(\sigma) = k \ll N/L_{\min}\), then the implementation contains redundancy: lines that are not justified by the idea's geometry. These lines are either boilerplate, duplication, or features that extend beyond the original idea's support.

This correspondence gives a principled answer to "how large should the codebase be?" The answer is not a target number — it is the covering dimension of the idea, multiplied by the minimum per-module implementation cost. An idea with \(\mathrm{covdim}(\sigma) = 10\) and \(L_{\min} = 1500\) naturally justifies 15,000 lines. Asking for more is padding; asking for fewer is truncating.

10.4 Compositional Ideation: The Tower of Domain Sites

The full ideation engine operates on a tower of domain compositions, not a fixed pair. Starting from the primary domain \(\mathcal{D}_1\) and problem \(p\):

  1. Level 1 (pairwise): For each candidate partner domain \(\mathcal{D}_2\), compute \(H^1(\{\mathcal{D}_1, \mathcal{D}_2\}, \mathcal{S})\) in the fiber over \(p\). Evaluate covering dimension of each class. If \(\mathrm{covdim} < k_{\min}\) (the idea is too small), the pairing is insufficient.
  2. Level 2 (triple): For the best level-1 partner \(\mathcal{D}_2\), consider adding a third domain \(\mathcal{D}_3\). Compute \(H^2(\{\mathcal{D}_1, \mathcal{D}_2, \mathcal{D}_3\}, \mathcal{S})\). If the triple composition produces classes with higher covering dimension than the best pairwise class, the third domain enriches the idea — it adds components that could not exist without all three domains present.
  3. Level \(n\): Continue adding domains as long as each addition increases the covering dimension. Stop when the marginal gain falls below a threshold — this is the saturation point of the composition.
Theorem 10.3 — Compositional Saturation

Let \(\sigma_n \in H^{n-1}(\{\mathcal{D}_1, \ldots, \mathcal{D}_n\}, \mathcal{S})\) be the best idea at the \(n\)-fold level. The sequence \(\mathrm{covdim}(\sigma_1) \leq \mathrm{covdim}(\sigma_2) \leq \cdots\) is non-decreasing and bounded above by the total number of sub-domains across all composed domain sites. The composition saturates at the smallest \(n^*\) where

\[ \mathrm{covdim}(\sigma_{n^*+1}) - \mathrm{covdim}(\sigma_{n^*}) \;<\; \epsilon \]

for a threshold \(\epsilon > 0\). At saturation, adding more domains does not meaningfully increase the scale of the idea. Empirically, \(n^* \in \{2, 3, 4\}\) for most practical problems, meaning 2–4 domains are composed before saturation.

The tower gives the ideation engine a natural stopping criterion for domain composition. Rather than fixing \(n = 2\), the engine climbs the tower until saturation, composing exactly as many domains as the idea's geometry requires. A problem that needs 3 domains gets 3; a problem that is fully served by 2 gets 2. The geometry decides, not a parameter.

10.5 Scale-Aware Ideation: Filtering for Substantial Ideas

The covering dimension gives a principled filter for idea scale. The full useful-novelty criterion from Theorem 9.1 is now augmented with a scale condition:

Theorem 10.4 — Useful, Novel, and Substantial

A section \(\sigma\) is a useful, novel, and substantial idea for problem \(p\) if and only if:

\[ \underbrace{[\sigma] \neq 0}_{\text{novel}} \;\;\wedge\;\; \underbrace{\sigma_p \neq 0}_{\text{useful}} \;\;\wedge\;\; \underbrace{\mathrm{covdim}(\sigma) \geq k_{\min}}_{\text{substantial}} \]

where \(k_{\min}\) is the minimum covering dimension for the desired implementation scale (e.g., \(k_{\min} = 10\) for a 15,000-line system at \(L_{\min} = 1500\)).

The first two conditions are Theorem 9.1. The third is new: it ensures the idea is not a small tweak dressed up as a large system. An idea with \(\mathrm{covdim}(\sigma) < k_{\min}\) genuinely does not have enough geometric structure to justify \(k_{\min} \cdot L_{\min}\) lines of code — any implementation at that scale would necessarily contain padding.

This is the geometric content of "make sure each line is useful." If the idea has covering dimension 10, then 10 modules of 1,500 lines each = 15,000 lines, and every line is justified by a distinct region of the idea's support. If you could write the same system in 3,000 lines, the idea's covering dimension is really 2, not 10 — and you should either find a richer idea or accept the smaller scale.

📋

The tower of compositions, covering dimension, and scale-aware filtering work together. The tower determines how many domains to compose. The covering dimension of the resulting idea determines the natural implementation scale. The scale filter ensures we only pursue ideas that justify the target codebase size. The result: an idea whose implementation scale is a consequence of its geometric structure, not an imposed target.

11. The Geometry of Mathematical Discovery

The judgment-geometric account of mathematical ideation is not merely a change of language. It is a change of questions. Instead of asking "what might connect these two fields?" — an open-ended question with no principled stopping criterion — it asks "what are the sections of the theorem presheaf on the fiber product of these two field coordinates that do not lie in the image of either restriction map?" This is a mathematical question, and it has mathematical answers.

The sheaf-theoretic framework contributes four precise tools:

  1. The site topology tells you which field pairings are likely to have rich overlaps — which pairings are worth exploring.
  2. The cohomology tells you what kind of theorems to look for at each overlap — specifically, sections in the complement of both restriction-map images.
  3. The trust algebra tells you how confident to be in each discovered proposition and provides a structured path from LLM conjecture to Lean-verified theorem.
  4. The descent condition tells you when a synthesis is complete — when the bridge theorems are consistent with both parent fields and the combined structure has no outstanding obstructions.

Together these tools transform mathematical ideation from an art — the mysterious process by which mathematicians "see connections" between fields — into a computation: the systematic traversal of the sections of a sheaf on a site, guided by cohomological invariants and tracked by the judgment tuple at every step.
