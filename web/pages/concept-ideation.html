The Geometry of Ideation

How sheaf theory turns mathematical discovery into a descent problem — fields as a site, theorems as sections, novelty as a cohomological condition, and the tournament as a gluing computation.

🕑 35 min read Core Concept
📋
On this page: Fields as a site · Theorems as sections · Novelty as cohomology · Tournament as descent · Metaphors as morphisms · Propositions as judgment 8-tuples · Worked example · Why geometry beats random combination

Mathematical ideation — the act of conjecturing genuinely new results by connecting ideas across fields — has historically resisted formalization. It appears to require intuition, serendipity, and deep familiarity with disparate bodies of mathematics. JuGeo's ideation engine replaces this impressionistic account with a precise geometric one: given a catalog of mathematical fields equipped with analogy morphisms between them, new theorems are exactly the sections of a presheaf that live on overlaps but not in the image of any single restriction map. This is a cohomological condition, and the machinery of Grothendieck topologies gives us the tools to compute it systematically.

This page develops the conceptual framework from first principles. We describe the ideation site, define local sections and bridge theorems, characterize novelty as a class in \(H^1\), and show how JuGeo's binary tournament is structurally a descent computation. We close with a fully worked example — Poisson Geometry \(\times\) Infinity Categories — tracing each discovered proposition back to its geometric origin.

1. Mathematical Fields as a Site

JuGeo maintains a catalog of 128 mathematical fields. The naive view treats these as a flat list — a menu from which a user selects two fields to pair. The geometric view treats them as the objects of a category, and that categorical structure is what makes systematic discovery possible.

1.1 The Field Category

Define the field category \(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{math}}\) as follows. Objects are mathematical fields:

\[ \Ob(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{math}}) = \{\text{Type Theory},\; \text{Poisson Geometry},\; \text{Infinity Categories},\; \text{Homotopy Theory},\; \ldots\} \]

Morphisms are analogy arrows — structure-preserving maps that witness a meaningful similarity between fields:

\[ \Mor(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{math}}) = \{\text{analogy arrows between fields}\} \]

An analogy arrow \(f : F_1 \to F_2\) exists when there is a faithful translation of the core concepts of \(F_1\) into the language of \(F_2\). The strength of \(f\) — a number in \([0,1]\) — measures how faithful this translation is: how many of \(F_1\)'s theorems survive transport to \(F_2\) without essential loss. Composition of analogy arrows is transitive analogy: if symplectic geometry analogizes to Poisson geometry, and Poisson geometry analogizes to Lie algebras, then there is a (weaker) composed analogy from symplectic geometry to Lie algebras.

1.2 The Grothendieck Topology

The field category acquires a Grothendieck topology \(J\) by declaring when a collection of fields covers a field. Informally: a field \(F\) is covered by a family \(\{F_i\}_{i \in I}\) when every result in \(F\) can be explained by — or transported from — the combined knowledge of the \(F_i\).

Definition 1.1 — Ideation Site

The ideation site is the pair \((\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{math}},\, J)\) where \(J(F)\) consists of all sieves \(S\) on \(F\) such that for every theorem \(\varphi \in F\), there exists \(f : F_i \to F\) in \(S\) and a theorem \(\psi \in F_i\) with \(f(\psi) = \varphi\).

In other words, a sieve covers \(F\) if and only if every theorem in \(F\) has a preimage in some member of the sieve under the transport map \(f\).

This topology is natural. The Weinstein Splitting Theorem, a result in Poisson Geometry, is covered by its connections to symplectic geometry (local normal form), Lie theory (the symmetry reduction), and foliation theory (the leaf structure). A result in algebraic topology is covered by its connections to homotopy theory, category theory, and differential geometry. The covers are not artificial — they reflect the actual mathematical genealogy of the results.

1.3 Sheaves on the Ideation Site

With the site in hand, we can define sheaves. The key sheaf is the theorem presheaf \(\mathcal{T}\): for each field \(F\), the set \(\mathcal{T}(F)\) consists of all theorems (and conjectures, definitions, and lemmas) that legitimately belong to \(F\). The restriction maps \(\mathcal{T}(F) \to \mathcal{T}(F')\) along an analogy arrow \(f : F' \to F\) are the theorem transport maps: they carry a theorem stated in the language of \(F\) into the language of \(F'\), if such a translation is faithful.

The sheaf condition for \(\mathcal{T}\) on the covering \(\{F_i \to F\}\) is exactly:

\[ \mathcal{T}(F) \;\xrightarrow{\;\sim\;}\; \varprojlim\!\left(\prod_{i} \mathcal{T}(F_i) \;\rightrightarrows\; \prod_{i,j} \mathcal{T}(F_i \times_{\mathcal{F}} F_j)\right) \]

This says that a theorem over \(F\) is completely determined by its restrictions to the covering fields, provided those restrictions are mutually compatible on all pairwise overlaps. The pairwise overlaps \(F_i \times_{\mathcal{F}} F_j\) — the fiber products in the field category — are exactly the bridge regions between fields.

2. Theorems as Sections

2.1 Local Sections

A theorem in a mathematical field is a local section of \(\mathcal{T}\) at that field's coordinate. Consider the following examples:

  • The Weinstein Splitting Theorem (every Poisson manifold is locally a product of a symplectic manifold and a Poisson manifold with zero rank at a point) is a section \(\sigma_{\mathrm{WS}} \in \mathcal{T}(\text{Poisson Geometry})\).
  • The Joyal Model Structure (the category of simplicial sets carries a model structure whose fibrant objects are quasi-categories) is a section \(\sigma_{\mathrm{JMS}} \in \mathcal{T}(\text{Infinity Categories})\).
  • The Homotopy Hypothesis (\(\infty\text{-groupoids} \simeq \text{spaces}\)) is a section in \(\mathcal{T}(\text{Homotopy Theory}) \cap \mathcal{T}(\text{Infinity Categories})\).

Each local section lives at a specific field coordinate. The information in the section includes the statement of the theorem, its proof strategy, its known applications, and the trust level assigned to it by JuGeo's trust algebra (typically VERIFIED for classical results, COPILOT_SUGGESTED for LLM-generated conjectures).

2.2 Bridge Theorems as Sections on Overlaps

A bridge theorem is a genuinely new result connecting two fields. In the sheaf language, it is a section that lives on the overlap — the fiber product — of two field coordinates. It exists precisely because the analogy morphism between the fields creates an overlap region in which both fields' structure is simultaneously present.

Definition 2.1 — Bridge Theorem
\[ \text{Bridge theorem} \;\in\; \mathcal{T}(U_i \times_{\mathcal{F}} U_j) \]

A bridge theorem is a section of the theorem presheaf on the fiber product \(U_i \times_{\mathcal{F}} U_j\) of two field coordinates. Its restriction to \(U_i\) (respectively \(U_j\)) is the "projection" of the bridge theorem onto the language and concepts of field \(i\) (respectively \(j\)).

Not every section on an overlap is a bridge theorem in the interesting sense. If \(\sigma \in \mathcal{T}(U_i \times_{\mathcal{F}} U_j)\) is already in the image of \(\res_{U_i}\) — that is, if it is the restriction of a section that already existed at \(U_i\) — then it is not new: it is just an old theorem from \(F_i\) translated into the language of \(F_j\). The genuinely new bridge theorems are those that are not in the image of any restriction map. This is the cohomological condition developed in §3.

2.3 Restriction Maps and Theorem Transport

The restriction map \(\res^{U_i}_{U_i \times U_j} : \mathcal{T}(U_i) \to \mathcal{T}(U_i \times_{\mathcal{F}} U_j)\) is concretely realized as the theorem transport along the analogy morphism \(f_{ij} : U_i \times_{\mathcal{F}} U_j \to U_i\). Given a theorem \(\varphi\) stated in the language of \(F_i\), the transport \(f_{ij}^*(\varphi)\) is the same theorem restated in the joint language of \(F_i\) and \(F_j\) — with the concepts of \(F_j\) available to clarify, specialize, or generalize the original statement.

Transport is not always possible. If the analogy arrow \(f_{ij}\) is weak (low strength), the translation may not be faithful, and the restriction map is partially defined. When the restriction map is undefined on \(\varphi\), the theorem \(\varphi\) cannot be transported, which means the overlap region is richer than either parent field alone — there are things happening at the overlap that neither field can explain individually. Those things are the bridge theorems.

3. Novelty as a Cohomological Condition

3.1 The Cech Complex of the Ideation Cover

Fix two fields \(F_i\) and \(F_j\) with analogy arrow \(f_{ij}\). The overlap \(U_{ij} = F_i \times_{\mathcal{F}} F_j\) is the bridge region. We have restriction maps

\[ \mathcal{T}(F_i) \;\xrightarrow{\;\res^{i}_{ij}\;}\; \mathcal{T}(U_{ij}) \;\xleftarrow{\;\res^{j}_{ij}\;}\; \mathcal{T}(F_j) \]

The image of \(\res^{i}_{ij}\) consists of all theorems at the overlap that can be "explained" by \(F_i\) — they are just translated parent theorems. Similarly for \(\res^{j}_{ij}\). The cokernel of both maps simultaneously is the space of genuinely novel bridge theorems:

\[ \mathrm{Novel}(U_{ij}) \;=\; \mathcal{T}(U_{ij}) \big/ \left(\im(\res^i_{ij}) + \im(\res^j_{ij})\right) \]

This quotient is what lives in \(H^1\). A section \(\sigma \in \mathcal{T}(U_{ij})\) is novel — in the sense that it represents genuinely new mathematical content — if and only if its image in \(\mathrm{Novel}(U_{ij})\) is nonzero.

Theorem 3.1 — Novelty Criterion

A section \(\sigma \in \mathcal{T}(U_{ij})\) is a novel bridge theorem if and only if

\[ \sigma \;\notin\; \im(\res^i_{ij}) \;\cup\; \im(\res^j_{ij}) \]

That is, \(\sigma\) is not the restriction of any theorem from either parent field. In terms of the \(\check{\mathrm{C}}\)ech complex of the cover \(\{F_i, F_j\} \to F_{\mathrm{bridge}}\), the novelty class of \(\sigma\) is

\[ [\sigma] \;\in\; H^1\!\left(\{F_i, F_j\},\, \mathcal{T}\right) \;\setminus\; \{0\} \]

The \(\check{\mathrm{C}}\)ech 1-cocycle condition collapses here to: on the triple overlap \(U_{ij} \times U_{ij}\) (the overlap of the overlap with itself), the section satisfies the cocycle identity trivially since the overlap is a single object. Nontriviality of \([\sigma]\) is exactly the statement that \(\sigma\) is not a coboundary — not of the form \(\res^i_{ij}(\tau) - \res^j_{ij}(\tau')\) for any \(\tau \in \mathcal{T}(F_i),\, \tau' \in \mathcal{T}(F_j)\).

3.2 Why Cross-Tradition Pairings Are Richer

The novelty of the overlap depends critically on the distance between the two fields in the field category. Fields from the same tradition — say, symplectic geometry and contact geometry, both differential-geometric — have overlaps where the restriction maps are nearly surjective. Almost every section at the overlap is the restriction of something already known in one of the parent fields. The \(H^1\) class is small.

Fields from entirely different traditions — differential geometry and higher category theory, or combinatorics and homological algebra — have overlaps where the restriction maps are far from surjective. The concepts of one field have no natural image in the other, so the overlap contains sections that genuinely cannot be explained by either parent. These are the interesting bridge theorems, and they live in a large \(H^1\). This is why JuGeo preferentially pairs fields from different traditions: it is selecting for large \(H^1\), which is where the mathematical gold is.

💡

The 128-field catalog is not a menu — it is a metric space. The distance between two fields in the category (measured by the minimum number of analogy arrows needed to connect them) is inversely proportional to the triviality of their overlap. The ideation engine uses this metric to bias the tournament bracket toward cross-tradition pairings, maximizing the expected size of the \(H^1\) at each round.

3.3 Higher Cohomology and Multi-Field Bridges

The framework extends naturally to \(H^n\) for \(n \geq 2\). A \(H^2\) class on a triple overlap \(U_{ijk}\) represents a result that requires all three fields simultaneously — it cannot be recovered from any pair. Concretely:

\[ H^2\!\left(\{F_i, F_j, F_k\},\, \mathcal{T}\right) \]

captures theorems that are three-way bridge results. JuGeo's tournament structure is designed to find \(H^1\) classes efficiently (the binary tournament), but the synthesized fields it produces in later rounds become the \(F_i\) for the next round's \(H^1\) computation — effectively computing \(H^2\) and beyond through iterated \(H^1\) computations. This is the mathematical content of the recursive structure of the tournament.

4. The Tournament as a Descent Computation

JuGeo's ideation engine runs a binary tournament over the 128 fields. At each round, fields are paired, the pairing is evaluated by an LLM judge, and the winning pair is merged into a synthesized field that advances to the next round. The tournament terminates when one field remains — the "grand synthesis" of all fields.

This is not a metaphor for a mathematical process. It is a descent computation in the precise sheaf-theoretic sense.

4.1 The Descent Diagram

Recall that sheaf descent says: a section over a covered object \(U\) exists if and only if local sections exist on each \(U_i\) and they agree on all pairwise intersections \(U_i \times_U U_j\). The tournament instantiates this as follows:

ROUND 1 — Local Sections Start with theorems in individual fields
Each of the 128 fields contributes its known theorems as local sections \(\sigma_i \in \mathcal{T}(F_i)\). These are the starting points: well-understood results in their home fields, each with trust level VERIFIED or COPILOT_SUGGESTED depending on whether they are classical or LLM-generated.
ROUND 2 — Overlap Check Find bridge theorems at pairwise intersections
Fields are paired. For each pair \((F_i, F_j)\), the engine computes the fiber product \(U_{ij} = F_i \times_{\mathcal{F}} F_j\) and searches for sections \(\sigma_{ij} \in \mathcal{T}(U_{ij})\) that are not in the image of either restriction map. These are the bridge theorem candidates. The LLM evaluates each candidate on six dimensions, computing a novelty score that estimates the size of the \(H^1\) class.
ROUND 3 — Gluing Merge compatible pairs into synthesized fields
If the overlap is rich enough — the novelty score exceeds the configured threshold — the pair \((F_i, F_j)\) is glued into a synthesized field \(F_{ij}\). The synthesized field inherits all the theorems from both parents plus the new bridge theorems discovered at the overlap. This is the gluing step of sheaf descent: from compatible local sections \((\sigma_i, \sigma_j, \sigma_{ij})\), we construct a global section \(\sigma \in \mathcal{T}(F_{ij})\).
ROUND 4 — Recursion Repeat until one field remains
The synthesized fields \(F_{ij}\) become the new local sections for the next round. The tournament repeats: pairs of synthesized fields are combined, new bridge theorems are found at their overlaps, and the winners are glued. Each round computes \(H^1\) at a higher level of abstraction — effectively computing \(H^2, H^3, \ldots\) of the original field category. The final field is the "global section" of the entire discovery process.

4.2 The Judging Step as Gluing Condition

The LLM judge evaluates each pairing on six dimensions:

Leverage — how much new mathematics does the pairing unlock?
Metaphor Richness — how many analogy arrows connect the two fields?
Transportability — can theorems be faithfully translated between fields?
Proof Density — does the overlap contain enough structure to support proofs?
Novelty — is the bridge content outside the image of both restriction maps?
Geometry Fit — does the pairing fit the judgment-geometric framework?

These six dimensions are not ad hoc. They are precisely the conditions that must hold for the gluing step of descent to succeed:

  • Leverage and Proof Density measure whether the fiber product \(U_{ij}\) has enough sections — whether the presheaf value at the overlap is rich. Without them, the global section produced by gluing is vacuous.
  • Transportability measures how well-defined the restriction maps are. If \(\res^i_{ij}\) is poorly defined, the compatibility condition at the overlap is ambiguous, and the gluing may be inconsistent.
  • Novelty directly measures the \(H^1\) class. A high novelty score means the overlap sections are far from the images of the restriction maps — the synthesis is generating genuinely new mathematics.
  • Metaphor Richness measures the density of analogy arrows, which determines the site topology. More arrows means a richer covering, which means descent is more likely to succeed.
  • Geometry Fit checks global coherence: that the combined structure of \(F_i \cup F_j\) fits within the judgment-geometric framework and can be represented as a consistent site.

A pairing "advances" in the tournament if and only if its combined score crosses a threshold — which is exactly the condition that the local sections on \(F_i\) and \(F_j\) are compatible enough to glue into a coherent global section on \(F_{ij}\). The tournament is descent.

5. Metaphors as Morphisms

The MetaphorFinder is JuGeo's subsystem for discovering analogy arrows between fields. Concretely, it takes two field descriptions and produces a list of structure-preserving translations — each one a morphism in \(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{math}}\).

5.1 The Three Morphism Kinds

Analogy arrows are typed according to how faithful the translation is:

  • ANALOGY — a loose structural similarity. The concepts in \(F_1\) "remind one of" the concepts in \(F_2\), but the translation is not fully rigorous. Strength typically in \([0.3, 0.6]\).
  • EMBEDDING — a faithful translation in one direction. The concepts of \(F_1\) embed into \(F_2\) without loss, but \(F_2\) contains structure that has no counterpart in \(F_1\). Strength typically in \([0.6, 0.85]\).
  • ISOMORPHISM — a fully faithful bilateral translation. Every concept and theorem in \(F_1\) corresponds to one in \(F_2\) and vice versa. This is the categorical equivalence of fields. Strength typically in \([0.85, 1.0]\).

For the Poisson Geometry \(\times\) Infinity Categories pairing, the MetaphorFinder discovers the following analogy arrows:

A Poisson bracket \(\{f, g\}\) is an algebraic structure measuring non-commutativity on functions
⟶ ANALOGY
The homotopy coherence data in an \(\infty\)-category measuring non-strict commutativity of compositions
Symplectic leaves of a Poisson manifold — the minimal submanifolds carrying genuine symplectic structure
⟶ ANALOGY
Connected components of the space of objects in an \(\infty\)-groupoid — the minimal units of equivalence
A Poisson map \(f : (M, \pi_M) \to (N, \pi_N)\) preserving the Poisson structure
⟶ EMBEDDING
A functor of \(\infty\)-categories preserving the simplicial set structure and composition up to homotopy
The Lichnerowicz–Poisson cohomology \(H^*_\pi(M)\) measuring deformations of the Poisson structure
⟶ ANALOGY
The Hochschild cohomology of an \(A_\infty\)-algebra measuring deformations of the multiplication

5.2 Metaphor Strength and Site Density

The strength \(s(f)\) of an analogy arrow \(f : F_1 \to F_2\) is not just a quality measure — it determines the covering density in the Grothendieck topology. Specifically, the covering sieves in \(J(F)\) are filtered by the total strength of the morphisms in the sieve:

\[ \text{sieve } S \in J(F) \iff \sum_{f \in S} s(f) \;\geq\; \tau \]

where \(\tau\) is the coverage threshold (default 0.7). A collection of weak analogies can jointly cover a field even if no single analogy is strong enough alone. This is the sheaf-theoretic analog of "the whole is more than the sum of its parts" — five weak analogies collectively provide a richer bridge than one strong embedding.

The Poisson \(\times\) Infinity pairing has four analogy arrows with strengths approximately \([0.72, 0.68, 0.81, 0.65]\), for a total strength of \(2.86\), well above the threshold. The covering is dense, the fiber product is well-defined, and the descent computation can proceed.

6. Propositions as Judgment 8-Tuples

Every proposition discovered by the ideation engine is recorded as a complete judgment in the JuGeo sense. A judgment is an 8-tuple \(J = (c, \varphi, A, E, O, B, T, \Pi)\) whose components carry the full mathematical and epistemic content of the discovered result.

Definition 6.1 — Ideation Judgment
\[ J \;=\; (c,\; \varphi,\; A,\; E,\; O,\; B,\; T,\; \Pi) \]
Component Symbol Description for ideation judgments
Coordinate c The field coordinate where the theorem lives, e.g. poisson_geometry × infinity_categories. For bridge theorems this is always a fiber product coordinate, not a single-field coordinate.
Formula \(\varphi\) The statement of the theorem or conjecture, in natural mathematical language or in JuGeo's formal assertion language when a formalization is available.
Assertion type A One of THEOREM, LEMMA, DEFINITION, CONJECTURE, or BRIDGE_THEOREM. Bridge theorems are the novel sections in \(H^1\); conjectures are unverified candidates.
Evidence E The evidence bundle: LLM-generated proof sketch, novelty score (estimated \(H^1\) class size), plausibility score from fast solver checks, and any analogical precursors that motivated the conjecture.
Open obligations O The proof obligations that remain unmet. For a COPILOT_SUGGESTED theorem, the open obligation is the full formal proof. For a LEAN_VERIFIED theorem, \(O = \emptyset\).
Obstructions B Known counterexamples or contradictions. If the theorem conflicts with a verified result in the ecology, the obstruction is recorded here and the judgment is flagged for review. An empty \(B\) means no known obstructions.
Trust level T One of COPILOT_SUGGESTED (LLM-generated, unverified), SOLVER_CHECKED (fast symbolic check passed), or LEAN_VERIFIED (full Lean 4 proof exists). Trust is monotone-upgradable but never downgraded without explicit obstruction evidence.
Provenance \(\Pi\) Complete audit trail: which tournament round discovered this theorem, which parent fields \(F_i\) and \(F_j\) were paired, which metaphors motivated the pairing, and the full LLM generation trace. Provenance enables reproducibility and supports trust upgrade.

The judgment tuple is not just bookkeeping. It encodes the geometric structure of the discovery:

  • The coordinate \(c\) records where in the field site the section lives.
  • The evidence \(E\) includes the novelty score, which is the numerical estimate of the \(H^1\) class — the "distance" from both parent images.
  • The open obligations \(O\) track the remaining gap between COPILOT_SUGGESTED and LEAN_VERIFIED trust — the proof burden that would elevate the section from a conjecture to a theorem.
  • The provenance \(\Pi\) records the descent path: the sequence of restriction maps and gluing steps that led to this section, enabling verification that the section is genuinely at the claimed coordinate and not a disguised transport from a parent.

7. Worked Example: Poisson Geometry × Infinity Categories

We trace a complete ideation run pairing Poisson Geometry with Infinity Categories, from the initial field sites through the metaphor discovery, tournament evaluation, and final proposition bundle.

7.1 The Local Sections

The engine begins with the known local sections at each field coordinate. At Poisson Geometry:

  • \(\sigma_1 = \) Weinstein Splitting Theorem (local normal form)
  • \(\sigma_2 = \) Poisson Cohomology long exact sequence
  • \(\sigma_3 = \) Lie algebroid structure on the cotangent bundle \(T^*M\)
  • \(\sigma_4 = \) Moser trick for Poisson structures (deformation equivalence)

At Infinity Categories:

  • \(\tau_1 = \) Joyal Model Structure on simplicial sets
  • \(\tau_2 = \) Lurie's Straightening/Unstraightening equivalence
  • \(\tau_3 = \) Adjoint functor theorem for \(\infty\)-categories

The most productive is the observation that both fields have a notion of "local structure that fails to be globally trivial in an interesting way": symplectic leaves in Poisson geometry are not globally a product decomposition, and the local sections of an \(\infty\)-categorical fibration are not globally trivial (the Grothendieck construction precisely measures this nontriviality). This suggests that Poisson cohomology and the cohomology of fibrations should be connected.

7.4 Novelty Analysis

The first three propositions have novelty scores above 0.85, confirming they are not in the image of either restriction map. To see this concretely:

  • The Poisson Fibration Theorem is not a restriction from Poisson Geometry: the notion of an "\(\infty\)-categorical fibration structure" is not available in the language of Poisson Geometry alone. It is not a restriction from Infinity Categories: the Poisson manifold and its cohomology class \([\pi]\) have no counterpart in a purely categorical setting. It genuinely lives at the overlap.
  • The Restriction Lemma has a lower novelty score (0.61) because its content — "the fiber of the fibration over a leaf is the symplectic structure on that leaf" — is close to the definition of the fibration and can nearly be recovered by restriction from the Poisson Fibration Theorem itself. It is at the boundary of the image of \(\res^i_{ij}\).

8. Why the Geometric Framework Beats Random Combination

Without the geometric framework, cross-field synthesis reduces to what we might call the cocktail party method: pick two fields that sound interesting together, ask an LLM to combine them, and hope something useful comes out. This works occasionally — combinatorics and topology, algebra and geometry, probability and analysis have all produced serendipitous hybrids — but it is unguided. There is no principled way to know in advance which pairings are promising, how to evaluate the results, or when the synthesis is complete.

The geometric framework answers all three questions:

8.1 Which Pairings Are Promising

The Grothendieck topology tells you which pairings have rich overlaps. Given the site \((\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{math}}, J)\) and a pair \((F_i, F_j)\), compute the fiber product \(U_{ij}\) and estimate the dimension of \(\mathcal{T}(U_{ij})\) relative to the images of the restriction maps. Pairs with large \(\dim(\mathcal{T}(U_{ij})) / (\dim(\im \res^i) + \dim(\im \res^j))\) are the promising ones. This ratio is the excess novelty fraction, and it can be estimated cheaply from the analogy arrow graph before running any expensive LLM computation.

Fields from far apart in the field category have high excess novelty fractions. Fields from the same tradition have low ones. The tournament bracket is constructed to maximize the sum of excess novelty fractions across all rounds — this is the combinatorial optimization problem that the bracket-design algorithm solves.

8.2 Where to Look for Novel Theorems

The cohomology tells you where to look. Specifically:

Proposition 8.1 — \(H^1\) Localization

Let \(\{F_i \to F\}\) be a covering family and \(\mathcal{T}\) the theorem presheaf. Novel bridge theorems are supported in the complement of the images of all restriction maps in \(\mathcal{T}(U_{ij})\). A systematic search for novel theorems can therefore be restricted to the orthogonal complement of \(\bigoplus_i \im(\res^{U_i}_{U_{ij}})\) in \(\mathcal{T}(U_{ij})\).

This is immediate from the definition of \(H^1\): the first cohomology group consists of 1-cocycles modulo 1-coboundaries. Coboundaries are exactly the sections in the image of restriction maps. So \(H^1\) sits in the complement of the coboundaries.

Practically: rather than asking an LLM "what theorems connect Poisson geometry and infinity categories?", JuGeo asks "what structures exist at the intersection that cannot be expressed in either field alone?" This is a sharper prompt that produces sharper results.

8.3 How Confident to Be in Each Result

The trust algebra tracks confidence through the judgment tuple's trust component \(T\). This is not just a tag — it is a value in the trust lattice with a precise semantics:

\[ \mathtt{NONE} \;\prec\; \mathtt{COPILOT\_SUGGESTED} \;\prec\; \mathtt{SOLVER\_CHECKED} \;\prec\; \mathtt{LEAN\_VERIFIED} \]

Trust is monotone: it can only increase as more evidence is gathered. The upgrade path from COPILOT_SUGGESTED to LEAN_VERIFIED for a bridge theorem is:

  1. Plausibility check: run fast solver checks on the statement, looking for immediate counterexamples. If no counterexample is found, upgrade to SOLVER_CHECKED (partial).
  2. Proof sketch formalization: have the research copilot expand the proof sketch into a structured outline with explicit lemmas. Each sub-lemma becomes a new judgment with its own trust level.
  3. Lean 4 verification: discharge the open obligations by producing a Lean 4 proof. When all sub-lemmas are verified, the parent bridge theorem upgrades to LEAN_VERIFIED.

8.4 When Synthesis Is Complete

The descent condition tells you when synthesis is complete. The tournament produces a synthesized field \(F_{ij}\) from the pair \((F_i, F_j)\). This synthesis is complete — in the sheaf-theoretic sense — if and only if:

  1. Every local section on \(F_i\) and \(F_j\) has been accounted for (either transported to the synthesis or recorded as an obstruction).
  2. The bridge theorems at the overlap \(U_{ij}\) are compatible with the local sections: their restrictions to each parent field agree with the known theorems in that field.
  3. There are no open obstructions: \(B = \emptyset\) for all bridge theorem judgments in the synthesis.

When these three conditions hold, the local sections on \(F_i\) and \(F_j\) plus the bridge theorems at \(U_{ij}\) form a complete gluing datum, and the descent condition is satisfied. The synthesized field \(F_{ij}\) is a well-defined object in the field category, and its theorem sheaf is the sheafification of the presheaf defined by the gluing data.

⚠

Partial synthesis and \(H^1\) obstructions. When the descent condition fails — when some bridge theorem's restriction to a parent field contradicts a known theorem in that field — the failure is recorded as a nonzero class in \(H^1(\mathcal{U}, \mathcal{T})\). This is not an error in the engine; it is information. The obstruction class tells you precisely which aspect of the two fields is incompatible and must be resolved before a consistent synthesis can exist. Often, resolving the obstruction requires discovering an additional lemma that mediates the conflict — a "bridge to the bridge."

9. The Geometry of Mathematical Discovery

The judgment-geometric account of mathematical ideation is not merely a change of language. It is a change of questions. Instead of asking "what might connect these two fields?" — an open-ended question with no principled stopping criterion — it asks "what are the sections of the theorem presheaf on the fiber product of these two field coordinates that do not lie in the image of either restriction map?" This is a mathematical question, and it has mathematical answers.

The sheaf-theoretic framework contributes four precise tools:

  1. The site topology tells you which field pairings are likely to have rich overlaps — which pairings are worth exploring.
  2. The cohomology tells you what kind of theorems to look for at each overlap — specifically, sections in the complement of both restriction-map images.
  3. The trust algebra tells you how confident to be in each discovered proposition and provides a structured path from LLM conjecture to Lean-verified theorem.
  4. The descent condition tells you when a synthesis is complete — when the bridge theorems are consistent with both parent fields and the combined structure has no outstanding obstructions.

Together these tools transform mathematical ideation from an art — the mysterious process by which mathematicians "see connections" between fields — into a computation: the systematic traversal of the sections of a sheaf on a site, guided by cohomological invariants and tracked by the judgment tuple at every step.
